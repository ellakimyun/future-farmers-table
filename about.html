About

Future food should start on a kitchen counter.

We design kits and workshops that make new ways of growing food small enough to try at home, and clear enough that a first-timer actually harvests something.

Why we exist

Most people want to try growing food. Most never start.

The hard part isn’t space or money. It’s not knowing which kit fits a small apartment, whether it’s too advanced, and whether it will actually grow. So every product here answers four questions before you buy: how much space, what level, what it costs, and how long until you eat something.

How it works

From box to first harvest

01

Choose a kit that fits

Filter by the space you have, your experience, your budget and how soon you want to eat something. Every card shows level and days to harvest up front.

02

Grow with guided lessons

Each kit unlocks short lessons that explain what the plant, fungus or culture is doing, step by step.

03

Harvest, then go further

Pick your first harvest, then come to a workshop to try the next field. If nothing sprouts in 21 days, we replace it.

Design notes

About this project

The Future Farmer’s Table is a concept store designed and built by Hayun Kim for SI 407 at the University of Michigan. Products, workshops and prices are fictional, and the cart and checkout never take payment.

Problem statement

How might we help first-time growers with little space quickly find a kit that fits their space, skill level and budget, and feel sure they’ll actually harvest something?

That question shaped the filters (level, space, price, time to harvest), the fit finder on the home page, the day-by-day harvest timeline on each kit, and the first-harvest promise.

What I learned from competitors

  • Auk: calm, product-first studio photography, so every kit here is shot on the same bone sweep and cards compare at a glance.
  • Click & Grow: filters with live counts. I added level and days to harvest, which their cards don’t show.
  • AeroGarden: a clear card hierarchy with a quick add. Here the add button only reacts to the button itself, never the whole card.

Moodboard

  • A24: editorial pacing and big, quiet type.
  • Nothing: precise, technical details such as mono day counters and small labels.
  • Figure and 1X: cinematic, warm photography of technology living in real homes.

Visual direction

Organic Futurism: greenhouse photography, translucent glass buttons, brushed-metal surfaces, Syne for headings and Geist for everything you read. Green is reserved for actions and status.
